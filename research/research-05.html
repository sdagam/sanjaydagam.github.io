Timing of the Afternoon NO₂ Minimum in TEMPO Hourly Observations:
An Evaluation Against Surface Monitors and Pandora

A full year of TEMPO satellite NO₂ compared against 399 EPA surface monitors and ground-based Pandora spectrometers — finding that the satellite reaches its summer afternoon low more than an hour before either ground instrument, and tracing the gap to the retrieval rather than to the atmosphere.

Plain-English Summary

Nitrogen dioxide in city air rises and falls on a daily rhythm — highest during the morning rush, lowest in mid-afternoon, when the warming ground stirs the air upward and sunlight breaks the gas apart. NASA's TEMPO satellite, launched in 2023, is the first instrument to watch North America every daylight hour, so that daily rhythm can now be followed from space. But a satellite measures the whole column of air above a point, while a ground monitor samples the air people actually breathe. Whether the two agree on when the daily low arrives had never been tested.

This study compared a full year of TEMPO data against 399 EPA ground monitors and against Pandora instruments, which measure the same column of air but from below. In summer, TEMPO places the afternoon low roughly an hour and a half before both — while the two ground instruments agree with each other. That pattern points to how the satellite converts its raw signal into a measurement, not to anything happening in the air. The disagreement grows as the sun sinks lower in the sky, which suggests where the problem lies.

The Research Gap

Three gaps this study addresses

Why this work is needed
1

Nobody had checked whether the clocks agree. A growing body of work compares TEMPO with surface monitors hour by hour, but all of it assumes the two instruments mark the daily cycle at the same time. No study had isolated the one moment where the two can be compared cleanly — the afternoon minimum.

2

The standard way of building daily cycles is biased. Clouds and a sun-angle screen cost TEMPO about 40% of its daylight hours, and not the same hours each day. Averaging every observation within each hour — the method used in published TEMPO and GEMS studies — mixes differences between days into the shape of the cycle itself.

3

A column and a concentration are different quantities. If satellite and monitor disagree, the column may genuinely behave differently from the air at ground level — or the retrieval may simply be wrong. Only an independent column measurement can separate the two, and ground-based Pandora spectrometers provide one.

Background

Why the timing is hard to pin down

NO₂ is a combustion pollutant linked to asthma, cardiovascular disease and mortality, and it is a main precursor to ground-level ozone and PM2.5. Its daily cycle in urban air is set by three things at once: the boundary layer deepening through the day and diluting what is below it, traffic emissions peaking morning and evening, and sunlight driving chemical loss. Together these produce a morning maximum and a well-defined afternoon minimum at the surface.

TEMPO (Tropospheric Emissions: Monitoring of Pollution) observes from geostationary orbit, sweeping the contiguous United States every daylight hour at roughly 2 × 4.5 km. It measures NO₂ along the slanted path of reflected sunlight, then divides by an air mass factor — which depends on a model's estimate of how NO₂ is distributed with height — to obtain a vertical column. Its recommended quality screen discards readings when the sun is more than 70° from overhead, so the observing window opens about two hours after sunrise and closes about two hours before sunset. The morning maximum therefore falls outside it entirely, and the afternoon minimum can be resolved only in spring and summer.

Pandora is a ground-based spectrometer that looks up and measures the same column of NO₂ that TEMPO measures from above — conceptually the same quantity, but arrived at without the satellite's retrieval assumptions. That makes the Pandonia Global Network the natural referee: where TEMPO and a surface monitor disagree, Pandora can say whether the column really behaves differently from the surface, or whether the satellite has it wrong.

A third study supplies the benchmark. Simulating the surface–column relationship, Harkey and Holloway (2024) found that a column weighted the way a satellite sees it — least sensitive near the ground — should reach its afternoon minimum roughly 45 minutes before the surface, while a column summed equally over all heights should reach it about 15 minutes after. This study tests those predictions with observations.

Methods

Building daily cycles from an incomplete record

The study uses TEMPO Level 2 tropospheric NO₂ (Version 3) for all of 2024, hourly NO₂ from the 399 EPA monitors reporting at least 75% of hours that year, and Pandonia Global Network retrievals at the 40 Pandora sites within 12 km of one of those monitors. Between 08:00 and 16:00 local time, only 60.8% of TEMPO's day-by-hour cells contain a valid retrieval — and a day observed at 10:00 may be clouded over at 14:00, so each hour is represented by a different set of days.

Rather than average within each hour, this study fits a two-way decomposition: every observation is modelled as that day's overall level plus the typical departure for that hour, with both estimated together from the observed values only. A day seen just in the afternoon is then compared with typical afternoon values instead of with its own mean, and incomplete days are kept rather than discarded.

The two methods were scored against a reference in a masking experiment. Surface monitors report about 96% of all hours, so their complete records give the true time of the minimum. Each surface record was then given TEMPO's exact gaps — removing 37% of the day-hours on average — and rebuilt by each method. Because both methods see the same masked data, any difference between them comes from the method alone.

Key Findings

Five findings, from method to mechanism

01
How the cycle is built changes the answer

In the masking experiment, hourly averaging placed the summer minimum about half an hour later than the reference from complete surface records; the two-way decomposition showed no detectable shift. Applied to TEMPO itself, the two methods disagree by 30 minutes or more at roughly a third of monitor-seasons, and in 9% hourly averaging invents a morning maximum that is not there.

02
The observing window rules out the morning

TEMPO's window is set by the 70° sun-angle screen, not by daylight. In 55–65% of monitor-seasons the column is highest at the very first observed hour, meaning the true maximum came earlier; plausible assumptions about when shift the estimated morning lag by 4–6 hours and reverse its sign. No morning timing can be reported, and the afternoon minimum is reliable only in spring and summer.

03
Nationally, TEMPO's minimum arrives early

Across all 399 monitors, the TEMPO column minimum precedes the surface minimum by 1.2–1.4 h in summer and 0.4–0.6 h in spring. The 0.76 h gap between the two seasons is statistically significant (p = 4.7 × 10⁻⁶). In fall and winter the window closes too soon after the minimum for a firm answer.

04
Pandora agrees with the surface; TEMPO does not

At the 21 summer sites where all three instruments resolve a minimum, TEMPO precedes the surface by 1.6 h (95% CI 0.8–2.5 h) and Pandora by 1.2–1.7 h depending on how the stratosphere is handled — while Pandora and the surface show no detectable difference (+0.07 h). Since Pandora measures the same column TEMPO does, the disagreement points to the satellite retrieval rather than to the atmosphere.

05
The gap follows the sun, not the clock

The TEMPO–Pandora difference grows as the sun sinks: +0.51 × 10¹⁵ molecules cm⁻² per unit of 1/cos(SZA) on summer afternoons (p < 0.001). It is largest whenever the sun is low — mornings included, when clock and sun run in opposite directions — so it tracks sun angle rather than time of day. Correcting TEMPO for it closes most or all of the summer gap, consistent with an air mass factor that leans too heavily on a model profile when little sunlight reaches the lowest air.

Limitations

What this study could not settle

  • It covers one year (2024) and one product version (V03). Version 4, released in September 2025, updates the assumed vertical profiles and corrects both a surface-pressure error and an overestimate of cloud fraction — it was not available when the analysis began.
  • The principal result rests on 21 summer Pandora pairs, and holds only where TEMPO resolves a minimum inside its observing window; at the six sites where the column was still falling at closing time, the lead is no longer clear.
  • The size of the lead depends on how the stratospheric column is removed from Pandora, ranging from 0.96 h to 1.93 h across the methods tested.
  • The sun-angle explanation is plausible but unproven: the fall slope runs the other way, one site (Whittier) carries much of the effect, and in the afternoon sun angle and clock time cannot be fully separated.
