Limits and Continuity: The Rigorous Foundation of Calculus

#133 of 156 July 18, 2026 Group 6 · Jul 2026–Jan 2027

Around 450 BC, a Greek philosopher named Zeno proposed a paradox that stumped mathematicians for two thousand years. To walk across a room, he argued, you must first walk halfway.

What Zeno Got Wrong

Zeno assumed that infinitely many steps must add up to an infinite total. But they don't have to. The distances he described — 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, ... — add up to exactly 1. The sum of infinitely many numbers can be a perfectly finite number. This seems obvious now, but it wasn't until mathematicians built a precise, airtight definition of what "approaches" and "sum of infinitely many terms" actually mean that anyone could prove it rigorously. That definition is the limit.

The Limit: What "Getting Closer" Really Means

The limit is the answer to the question: what value does a sequence of numbers approach as we take more and more steps? For Zeno's distances, each partial sum — 1/2, then 3/4, then 7/8, then 15/16 — gets closer and closer to 1, never quite reaching it but never stopping either. The limit is 1.

The formal definition (epsilon-delta): lim(x → a) f(x) = L means: For ANY tiny positive number ε (epsilon) you choose, there EXISTS a positive number δ (delta) such that: whenever x is within δ of a, f(x) is within ε of L. In plain English: no matter how precisely you demand f(x) to be close to L, we can guarantee it by making x close enough to a. It's a game — you name the precision, we find the closeness needed.

This might look like bureaucratic overkill for something that feels obvious. But "getting closer and closer" is deceptively slippery. What about a function that equals 0 everywhere except at exactly one point, where it equals 1? Does it "approach" 0 or 1? The epsilon-delta definition cuts through the ambiguity: it says exactly what "approach" means with mathematical precision, leaving no room for argument.

Continuity: No Teleportation Allowed

A function is continuous if it has no jumps — if the value you get at a point matches the limit approaching that point. In plain English: you can draw it without lifting your pencil. Temperature over the course of a day is continuous — it doesn't jump from 60°F to 90°F in an instant. A light switch is not continuous — it jumps from off to on with nothing in between.

The reason continuity matters in calculus is the Intermediate Value Theorem: if a continuous function is negative at one point and positive at another, it must equal zero somewhere in between. This seems obvious — but "obvious" isn't a proof. The rigorous definition of continuity makes this provable. Engineers use it to guarantee that a continuous function (like a stress measurement in a beam) that exceeds a safety threshold somewhere must have exceeded it gradually — there's a specific moment it first crossed the limit.

Why This Matters More Than It Seems

Isaac Newton and Gottfried Leibniz invented calculus in the 1600s. It worked brilliantly — it predicted planetary orbits, described fluid flow, explained optics. But for 150 years nobody could fully explain why it worked. The procedures gave correct answers, but the logical foundation was shaky. In the 1820s, Augustin-Louis Cauchy built that foundation using limits and the epsilon-delta definition. He showed that derivatives and integrals — the two pillars of calculus — could be defined precisely in terms of limits. The machinery that runs modern science and engineering was built on Zeno's two-thousand-year-old puzzle.

Other Applications

Limits aren't just foundational philosophy. They determine whether an infinite series converges (adds up to a finite answer) or diverges (grows without bound) — which matters everywhere from engineering computations to financial models.

Conclusion

Zeno proved that crossing a room requires infinitely many steps. He was right. The resolution — that infinitely many steps can take finite time — required a precise mathematical definition of what "approaches" means.
