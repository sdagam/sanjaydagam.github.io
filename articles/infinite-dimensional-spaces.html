Infinite-Dimensional Spaces: From Matrices to Functions

#137 of 156 August 15, 2026 Group 6 · Jul 2026–Jan 2027

When a musician plays middle C on a piano, the string doesn't just vibrate at one frequency — it vibrates at the fundamental frequency and at all of its harmonics simultaneously: twice the frequency, three times, four times, and so on, forever. The sound you hear is a superposition of infinitely many pure tones.

Linear Algebra's Secret Generality

In ordinary linear algebra, you work with vectors — lists of numbers like (3, -1, 4) — and matrices that transform them. The key operations are addition (add two vectors component by component) and scaling (multiply every component by a number). A vector space is any collection of objects where those two operations make sense and follow the usual rules.

Functions are such objects. You can add two functions: (f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x). You can scale a function: (2f)(x) = 2f(x). Functions form a vector space — an infinite-dimensional one, because describing a function completely requires specifying its value at infinitely many points. The "components" of a function are its values at each point, and there are infinitely many points.

Fourier Basis: The Natural Coordinates for Sound

Every finite-dimensional vector space has a basis — a set of reference vectors that can build any other vector by addition and scaling. For functions on an interval, a natural basis is the set of all sine and cosine waves at different frequencies: sin(x), cos(x), sin(2x), cos(2x), sin(3x), cos(3x), and so on forever.

Any function f(x) on an interval can be written as: f(x) = a₀ + a₁cos(x) + b₁sin(x) + a₂cos(2x) + b₂sin(2x) + ... The coefficients aₙ and bₙ are the "coordinates" of f in the Fourier basis. They tell you how much of each frequency is present. For the piano note: most coefficients are near zero. The large ones correspond to the fundamental and harmonics. This is exactly what a frequency spectrum analyzer displays.

This is the Fourier series — infinite-dimensional linear algebra in action. The basis vectors are the sine and cosine waves. The coordinates are the Fourier coefficients. Decomposing a function into its Fourier coefficients is exactly like expressing a vector in terms of basis vectors — just with infinitely many of them.

Inner Products: Measuring Angles Between Functions

In ordinary geometry, the dot product of two vectors tells you the angle between them. Perpendicular vectors have dot product zero. In function space, there's an analogue: the inner product of two functions f and g is the integral of their product over an interval. Two functions are orthogonal — perpendicular — when this integral is zero.

The sine and cosine waves in the Fourier basis are all orthogonal to each other. This is why Fourier coefficients can be extracted independently: integrate f(x) times sin(nx) over the interval, and you get only the coefficient bₙ — all the other terms vanish because they're orthogonal to sin(nx). The same trick that simplifies ordinary vector decomposition works in infinite dimensions.

Why Infinite Dimensions Are Manageable

You might think infinitely many dimensions would be unmanageable. In practice, the Fourier coefficients for most real-world signals shrink rapidly — the higher the frequency, the smaller the coefficient. You only need the first 10 or 50 or 100 terms to represent most signals accurately. This is why MP3 compression works: discard the tiny high-frequency coefficients, keep the large low-frequency ones, and the sound is perceptually identical. Infinite-dimensional mathematics, finite-dimensional computation.

Other Applications

Infinite-dimensional spaces are the natural home of quantum mechanics — the state of a quantum particle is a vector in an infinite-dimensional space called a Hilbert space (Article #154 will explore this). They're used in signal processing, image compression, solving partial differential equations, and machine learning with kernel methods.

Conclusion

Linear algebra doesn't stop at finite-dimensional vectors. Functions form vector spaces too — infinite-dimensional ones — and all the tools of linear algebra carry over: bases, coordinates, inner products, orthogonality. The Fourier basis decomposes any function into sine and cosine components, turning the complicated question "what does this function look like?" into "how much of each frequency does it contain?" From piano acoustics to MP3 files to quantum mechanics, infinite-dimensional linear algebra is the mathematical language of continuous data.
