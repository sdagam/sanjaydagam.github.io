Algebraic Graph Theory: Polynomials and Matrices of Graphs

#146 of 156 October 17, 2026 Group 6 · Jul 2026–Jan 2027

In the 1950s, chemists trying to predict the boiling points and chemical reactivity of organic molecules discovered something strange: certain mathematical properties of the molecule's structure — specifically, numbers derived from a matrix built from the pattern of chemical bonds — predicted physical properties far better than anyone expected. They weren't computing anything that seemed directly physical. They were doing linear algebra on a graph. The field that grew from these observations — algebraic graph theory — connects the abstract structure of networks to their concrete properties through algebra, and it's now used to design computer chips, analyze social networks, and prove results in pure mathematics.

The Adjacency Matrix

Any network can be encoded as a matrix. Number the nodes 1, 2, ..., n. Build an n×n matrix where entry (i,j) equals 1 if nodes i and j are connected by an edge, and 0 if they're not. This is the adjacency matrix A. Multiplying A by itself — computing A² — reveals something beautiful: the entry (A²)ᵢⱼ counts the number of walks of length 2 from node i to node j. A³ counts walks of length 3. The matrix powers encode the network's connectivity at every distance.

Adjacency Matrix A: Aᵢⱼ = 1 if edge (i,j) exists, 0 otherwise (A²)ᵢⱼ = number of walks of length 2 from i to j = Σₖ AᵢₖAₖⱼ (sum over all possible intermediate nodes k) (Aⁿ)ᵢⱼ = number of walks of length n from i to j Eigenvalues of A = the spectrum of the graph Largest eigenvalue λ₁ relates to the maximum degree Second eigenvalue λ₂ measures how well-connected the graph is

The Characteristic Polynomial

Every matrix has a characteristic polynomial: det(A - λI) = 0, whose roots are the eigenvalues. For graphs, this polynomial is called the graph's characteristic polynomial, and remarkably, many chemical properties of molecules correlate strongly with its coefficients. The constant term counts the number of perfect matchings (ways to pair up atoms). The sum of eigenvalues squared counts the number of edges. The characteristic polynomial is a fingerprint of the graph's structure.

Two different graphs can sometimes have the same characteristic polynomial — they're called cospectral graphs, and understanding when this happens is a deep open problem. But for most real molecules, the polynomial uniquely characterizes the structure and predicts properties accurately enough that chemists call eigenvalue-based molecular descriptors "topological indices" and use them routinely in drug design.

Expander Graphs: Algebraic Magic for Computer Science

An expander graph is a network that is both sparse (few edges per node) and highly connected (information can travel quickly across the whole graph). These seem contradictory — usually highly connected means many edges. Expanders achieve the balance, and they're extraordinarily useful: error-correcting codes, random number generators, and efficient communication networks can all be built from expanders.

The algebraic characterization: a graph is a good expander when its second-largest eigenvalue λ₂ is much smaller than its largest eigenvalue λ₁. The gap λ₁ - λ₂ is called the spectral gap, and it directly measures how quickly information (or a random walk) spreads across the network. Large spectral gap = good expander = fast mixing. This is the algebraic theorem that lets mathematicians prove that certain graphs are good expanders without simulating them.

Graph Isomorphism and the Limits of Algebra

One of the oldest problems in combinatorics is graph isomorphism: given two graphs, are they structurally identical (just with nodes relabeled)? If two graphs have different adjacency matrix spectra, they're definitely different. But cospectral graphs show that identical spectra don't guarantee identical structure. Algebraic methods — the most powerful tools available — can't fully solve graph isomorphism. Whether an efficient algorithm exists is a major open problem, sitting tantalizingly between the easy problems (P) and the hardest ones (NP-complete).

Conclusion

Algebraic graph theory translates the structure of networks into algebra — matrices, polynomials, eigenvalues — and uses algebraic tools to reveal structural properties. Matrix powers count paths. Characteristic polynomials fingerprint molecular structure. Spectral gaps measure connectivity for computer science applications. The field connects pure mathematics (polynomial algebra, linear algebra) to chemistry, computer science, and network analysis through the elegant idea that a network's structure is entirely captured by the algebra of its adjacency matrix.
