Sequences and Series: Convergence and the Meaning of Infinity

#136 of 156 August 8, 2026 Group 6 · Jul 2026–Jan 2027

Here is a sum that looks wrong: 1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + ... forever. What does it equal? Group the terms one way: (1-1) + (1-1) + ... = 0 + 0 + ... = 0. Group them another way: 1 + (-1+1) + (-1+1) + ... = 1 + 0 + 0 + ... = 1. An 18th-century mathematician named Guido Grandi claimed the answer was 1/2, since it's the average of 0 and 1. None of these answers is satisfying — and all of them are wrong in the same way. The series simply doesn't converge to any value. But figuring out exactly what that means, and how to tell convergent series from divergent ones, turned out to be one of the deepest problems in mathematics.

Sequences: The Building Blocks

A sequence is just a list of numbers in order: 1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, ... or 1, 2, 3, 4, ... The first thing you want to know about any sequence is whether it converges — whether the numbers settle toward a specific value — or diverges — whether they wander off to infinity or oscillate forever.

The first sequence above converges to 0: the terms keep getting smaller and approach zero. The second diverges: the numbers grow without bound. Grandi's sequence (1, -1, 1, -1, ...) diverges in a different way — it oscillates and never settles.

Series: Adding It All Up

A series is what you get when you add the terms of a sequence: 1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ... The way to make sense of this infinite sum is to look at partial sums — the sum of the first n terms — and ask whether those partial sums converge to a limit.

Geometric series: a + ar + ar² + ar³ + ... Partial sums: S₁ = a S₂ = a + ar = a(1 + r) S₃ = a(1 + r + r²) Sₙ = a(1 - rⁿ)/(1 - r) If |r| < 1, then rⁿ → 0 as n → ∞, so: S = a/(1 - r) ← the infinite sum converges to this For 1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + ...: a=1, r=1/2 S = 1/(1 - 1/2) = 2 Zeno's distances (1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ...): a=1/2, r=1/2 S = (1/2)/(1/2) = 1 ← you do cross the room.

Grandi's series has r = -1, and |-1| is not less than 1, so the formula doesn't apply — and indeed the series diverges. The condition |r| < 1 is what separates convergence from divergence for geometric series.

Tests for Convergence

For more complicated series, mathematicians developed a toolkit of convergence tests. The ratio test: if the ratio of consecutive terms approaches a value less than 1, the series converges. The comparison test: if your series is smaller term-by-term than a known convergent series, it converges too. These tests let you determine whether an infinite sum is meaningful without actually summing infinitely many terms.

One of the most surprising results: the harmonic series 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 + ... diverges, even though the terms go to zero. The terms shrink — but not fast enough. This was proved by Nicole Oresme in the 14th century, and it remains counterintuitive: you're adding smaller and smaller numbers, yet the sum grows without bound.

Why This Matters Everywhere

Scientists and engineers compute with infinite series constantly — not because they want to add infinitely many numbers, but because a finite partial sum gives an approximation as accurate as you need. The number e (approximately 2.718) is defined as the sum of 1 + 1 + 1/2 + 1/6 + 1/24 + ... The cosine function is computed as 1 - x²/2 + x⁴/24 - ... GPS receivers, financial models, and physics simulations all depend on knowing whether these approximations converge and how quickly.

Conclusion

Grandi's paradox — a series that seems to equal both 0 and 1 — reveals that infinite sums aren't automatically meaningful. The key concept is convergence: whether the partial sums settle toward a definite limit. Geometric series converge when the ratio is less than 1 in size. The harmonic series diverges despite its shrinking terms. Real analysis gave mathematicians the tools to tell the difference rigorously — not by intuition or clever grouping, but by precise definitions of limits. Every time a calculator computes a sine or cosine, it's using a convergent series, trusting that the math behind it is airtight.
