Green's Functions: Solving Differential Equations with Impulses

#144 of 156 October 3, 2026 Group 6 · Jul 2026–Jan 2027

In 1831, a self-taught English mathematician named George Green — who had spent most of his life running a mill — published a paper that barely anyone read at the time. It introduced a technique for solving differential equations by breaking them into the response to simple impulse inputs. Green died in 1841, nearly forgotten. Twenty years later, his paper was rediscovered and recognized as one of the most practically useful ideas in mathematical physics. Today, Green's functions are used to design MRI scanners, model earthquake propagation, analyze electrical circuits, and solve quantum scattering problems. The mill operator's forgotten paper became a cornerstone of applied mathematics.

The Key Idea: Superposition

Many physical systems satisfy linear differential equations: the equation of motion for a vibrating bridge, the equation for heat flow in a rod, the equation for electromagnetic wave propagation. Linear means the response to a combination of inputs is the combination of responses to each input separately. If input A produces response R_A and input B produces response R_B, then input (A + B) produces response (R_A + R_B).

Green's insight: any complicated input can be built from simple spike inputs — impulses that are zero everywhere except at one instant (or one location), where they're infinitely large but with finite total area. If you know the system's response to a single impulse at every possible location, you can reconstruct the response to any input by superposition.

The Green's Function

The Green's function G(x, s) is the system's response at location x to an impulse at location s. Once you have G, solving the differential equation for any source distribution f(x) becomes an integral:

If L is the differential operator (e.g., L = d²/dx² for the heat equation) and L[u] = f(x) is the equation to solve, then: Green's Function G(x,s) satisfies: L[G(x,s)] = δ(x-s) (the response to a unit impulse δ at location s) Solution for any source f(x): u(x) = ∫ G(x,s) f(s) ds In plain English: the response u at point x is the sum (integral) of all the individual impulse responses G(x,s), each weighted by how strong the source is at location s.

The Dirac delta function δ(x-s) — the mathematical impulse — is zero everywhere except at x = s, where it's infinitely large in such a way that its integral equals 1. It's not a function in the ordinary sense; it's a distribution. But it's the ideal unit impulse, and finding G for it unlocks the solution for all other sources.

MRI: Green's Functions in Medicine

An MRI scanner applies radio-frequency pulses to the body and measures the electromagnetic response of hydrogen atoms in different tissues. The relationship between the applied pulse and the measured signal is governed by a Green's function for the electromagnetic field equations in tissue. By carefully designing pulse sequences and measuring responses, the scanner effectively samples the Green's function of the body — and from that, reconstructs the 3D image of tissue density. Without Green's function theory, designing the pulse sequences that produce clean, artifact-free images would be impossible.

The Impulse Response of Circuits

In electrical engineering, every linear circuit has an impulse response — the output voltage when the input is a single brief spike. Once measured, the circuit's response to any input can be predicted by convolving that input with the impulse response. This is why measuring the impulse response is a standard technique for characterizing audio equipment, room acoustics, and communication channels. The impulse response is the circuit's Green's function.

Other Applications

Seismologists use Green's functions to model how earthquake waves propagate through the Earth's layers — predict the shaking at any location from a source at any depth. Quantum physicists use Green's functions to calculate scattering amplitudes — the probabilities of particles interacting in particle accelerators.

Conclusion

Green's function is the mathematical answer to "how does a system respond to a single point input?" Once you have that answer, superposition handles everything else — any complicated input is just a sum of point inputs, and the response is the corresponding sum of Green's functions. George Green's mill-operator insight — that linearity reduces any problem to a single fundamental response — runs through MRI machines, seismographs, audio equipment, and quantum physics. The most powerful ideas in mathematics are often the simplest.
