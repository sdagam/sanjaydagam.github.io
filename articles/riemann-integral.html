The Riemann Integral: What It Means to Integrate Rigorously

#139 of 156 August 29, 2026 Group 6 · Jul 2026–Jan 2027

Suppose you want to know the exact area under a curve — say, the total distance traveled by a car whose speed varied continuously over an hour. You could draw the speed-versus-time graph and estimate the area. You could divide it into rectangles and add them up. Make the rectangles thinner and thinner and your estimate improves. But when do you actually get the exact answer? At what point does "thinner and thinner rectangles" stop being an approximation and become the truth? This question — deceptively simple for smooth curves, devilishly subtle for others — took mathematicians until the 1850s to answer properly. The answer is the Riemann integral, and understanding it means understanding what integration really is.

The Rectangle Approximation

The idea behind integration is ancient: approximate the area under a curve using rectangles, then take the limit as the rectangles become infinitely thin. But what precisely does "take the limit" mean? Georg Friedrich Bernhard Riemann, building on Cauchy's definition of the limit, gave the first rigorous answer in 1854.

Riemann's approach: divide the interval from a to b into n subintervals. On each subinterval, draw a rectangle whose height equals the function value at some chosen point within that subinterval. Sum all the rectangle areas. This is the Riemann sum. If this sum approaches the same value regardless of how you choose those sample points — as long as the rectangles get thinner — then that value is the integral.

Riemann Sum: Divide [a, b] into n pieces of width Δx = (b-a)/n Pick any sample point xᵢ* in each piece Riemann Sum = Σᵢ f(xᵢ*) · Δx (height of rectangle × width, summed over all rectangles) The Integral: ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx = lim(n→∞) Σᵢ f(xᵢ*) · Δx This limit exists and is the same for any choice of sample points if and only if f is Riemann integrable on [a,b].

The "regardless of how you choose the sample points" part is the key. You could always pick the highest point in each rectangle, or always the lowest, or any point in between. If the sum converges to the same number no matter what — as the rectangles get infinitely thin — then the function is integrable and that number is the area.

What Can Go Wrong

Most functions you encounter in calculus class are Riemann integrable — continuous functions, functions with a finite number of jumps. But Riemann's definition reveals that some functions can't be integrated this way. Consider a function that equals 1 at every rational number and 0 at every irrational number. At any scale, every subinterval contains both rational and irrational points. The maximum-height rectangles always give sum 1; the minimum-height rectangles always give sum 0. The two never agree no matter how thin you make them. The Riemann integral of this function doesn't exist.

This "pathological" function seems like a mathematical curiosity. But it showed mathematicians that the Riemann integral — powerful as it is — has limits. It led to the development of the Lebesgue integral (Article #148), which handles such functions and forms the foundation of modern probability theory.

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Rigorously

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus says that differentiation and integration are inverse operations: the derivative of an integral gives back the original function. This theorem is what lets you evaluate integrals by finding antiderivatives rather than computing Riemann sums by brute force. Riemann's rigorous definition is what allows the theorem to be proved properly — not just demonstrated on examples but guaranteed for any integrable function.

Other Applications

The Riemann integral's framework underpins all of applied calculus: computing areas, volumes, work done by variable forces, probabilities from density functions, and Fourier coefficients. Every numerical integration method — the trapezoid rule, Simpson's rule — is a specific way of computing Riemann sums efficiently.

Conclusion

Integration was used successfully for two centuries before anyone could say precisely what it meant. Riemann's definition — the limit of rectangle sums regardless of sample point choice — gave integration its mathematical foundation. It explained not just how to compute integrals but what they are.
