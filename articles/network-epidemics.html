Network Epidemics Revisited: Exact Solutions on Special Graphs

#152 of 156 November 28, 2026 Group 6 · Jul 2026–Jan 2027

Early in the COVID-19 pandemic, epidemiologists used a simple model: everyone in the population has an equal chance of meeting everyone else. This produced predictions that were often badly wrong — underestimating the speed of early spread and overestimating the final size of outbreaks.

The SIR Model on Networks

The standard epidemic model has three states: Susceptible (can catch the disease), Infected (currently spreading it), and Recovered (immune). On a network, an infected person transmits to each susceptible neighbor independently with some probability β per time step, and recovers at rate γ. The question: starting from one infected person, how many total people get infected?

On a well-mixed population (everyone contacts everyone), the answer is clean: the total fraction infected R∞ satisfies a single transcendental equation. On a network, the answer depends intricately on who is connected to whom — the network structure. For most real networks (the internet, social networks, transport networks) no exact formula exists. But for special structures, the math works out exactly.

Trees: No Cycles, Exact Solutions

A tree is a network with no loops — every pair of nodes is connected by exactly one path. Family trees, corporate hierarchies, and some biological classification systems are tree-shaped. On a tree, an epidemic starting from the root spreads outward in one direction only — there's no way for the disease to "come around" and reinfect from another angle.

On a tree with branching number k (each node has k children): Each infected node transmits to each child independently with probability T = β/(β+γ) (T = transmissibility, the probability of transmission per contact) Probability epidemic spreads to at least n generations: Tⁿ Expected total infections from one seed: 1/(1 - kT) if kT < 1 (subcritical) → ∞ if kT ≥ 1 (supercritical) Critical threshold: kT = 1 In plain English: the epidemic explodes when transmissibility × branching number ≥ 1 Below 1: disease dies out. Above 1: exponential spread.

The threshold kT = 1 is a phase transition — a bifurcation in the epidemic's behavior (echoing Article #138). Below it, the disease infects only a small finite cluster and dies. Above it, it spreads to an infinite fraction of the network. This exact threshold, derived on trees, gives intuition that transfers to more complex networks.

Regular Graphs: Everyone Has the Same Degree

A k-regular graph is one where every node has exactly k neighbors — a perfectly uniform network. The symmetry allows exact solutions. The epidemic threshold on a k-regular graph is T = 1/k — the same as the tree formula, since the local neighborhood looks tree-like before cycles become important. The critical insight is that the early phase of an epidemic on any locally tree-like network (most real networks are approximately tree-like at short distances) follows the tree formula exactly, until the epidemic has grown large enough that cycles matter.

Star Networks: Super-Spreaders

A star network has one central hub connected to n leaves, with no edges among the leaves. This models a single super-spreader: someone connected to many others, who infects them all. On a star, the epidemic dynamics are dominated by whether the hub gets infected. If the hub is infected early, the disease spreads quickly to all leaves. If the hub escapes infection, the disease dies quickly with only a few leaf infections. The exact solution reveals that the epidemic threshold on a star is T = 1/√n — much lower than for a regular network, because a few contacts with the highly connected hub are enough to ignite a large outbreak.

The General Lesson

Exact solutions on special graphs reveal a general principle: the epidemic threshold depends on network structure through the ratio of mean degree squared to mean degree. For a regular graph (every node same degree k), this ratio is k, giving threshold 1/k. For a network with highly variable degrees — some super-spreaders with enormous numbers of contacts — this ratio is large, making the threshold very low. Networks with super-spreaders are dramatically more vulnerable to epidemics than uniform networks, even with the same average number of contacts.

Conclusion

Exact solutions to epidemic models on special networks — trees, regular graphs, stars — give mathematical insight that generalizes far beyond those special cases. The epidemic threshold (the line between dying out and exponential spread) depends on network structure through a simple ratio. Networks with super-spreaders cross this threshold at much lower transmissibility than uniform networks.
