Operator Theory: Linear Algebra in Infinite Dimensions

#143 of 156 September 26, 2026 Group 6 · Jul 2026–Jan 2027

Your phone's noise-canceling earbuds take in sound — a stream of numbers representing air pressure over time — and output a different stream of numbers: the canceling wave. Your camera's autofocus takes in a blurry image and outputs a sharpened one. Spotify's equalizer takes in audio at every frequency and turns the bass up. Each of these is a transformation: put something in, get something different out. When that something is a signal or a function — not just a list of numbers — the transformation is called an operator. Operator theory is the mathematics of these transformations, and it's the hidden engine behind signal processing, quantum mechanics, and every system that manipulates continuous data.

What Is an Operator?

You already know what a function is: put in a number, get out a number. f(3) = 9. An operator is one level up: put in a function, get out a function.

The derivative is an operator. Feed it the function x², get out 2x. The integral is an operator. Feed it a constant speed, get out the distance traveled. Convolution — the mathematical operation inside that noise-canceling earbud — is an operator. Each takes a function as input and produces a function as output.

These all share one key property: they're linear. Double the input, double the output. The output for two added inputs equals the sum of the outputs. This linearity is what makes operator theory a natural extension of linear algebra — the mathematics of matrices — to the world of functions.

An operator T is linear if: T[f + g] = T[f] + T[g] (output of sum = sum of outputs) T[c·f] = c·T[f] (output of scaled input = scaled output) Derivative D: D[x² + x] = D[x²] + D[x] = 2x + 1 ✓ linear Integral I: I[3f] = 3·I[f] ✓ linear Squaring S: S[f+g] = (f+g)² ≠ f² + g² ✗ NOT linear

Eigenfunctions: The Natural Frequencies

In linear algebra, an eigenvector is a vector that a matrix only stretches — it doesn't rotate or tilt, just scales. Operators have the same idea, but for functions. An eigenfunction of an operator is a function that the operator only scales — multiplies by a constant — rather than transforming into something different.

For the derivative operator, the eigenfunctions are exponential functions. Take the derivative of eˡˣ and you get λ·eˡˣ — exactly the same function, scaled by λ. The exponential is the derivative's eigenfunction. This is the deep reason why exponential functions appear in the solution of almost every differential equation: they're the natural modes, the functions that differential operators act on most simply.

For the noise-canceling earbud: the eigenfunctions of the convolution operator are pure sine waves at different frequencies. Each frequency passes through the earphone's filter unchanged in shape — only its volume is adjusted. This is why equalizers work: they independently scale each frequency, which is possible precisely because sine waves are eigenfunctions of convolution.

The Spectrum: More Than a List of Numbers

For a matrix, the eigenvalues are a finite list of numbers — one for each dimension. For an operator acting on functions, the set of eigenvalues — called the spectrum — can be much richer. It can be a continuous range of values, not just a discrete list.

This matters enormously in physics. An electron trapped in an atom has discrete energy levels — specific values only — because the relevant operator has a discrete spectrum. An electron moving freely through space can have any energy — because the same type of operator, without the boundary conditions of the atom, has a continuous spectrum. The difference between quantized atomic energy levels and continuous free-particle energies comes down to whether the operator's spectrum is discrete or continuous. Operator theory is what makes this precise.

Other Applications

MRI machines use operators called Fourier transforms to convert the radio frequency measurements from your body into an image — each frequency component processed independently because sine waves are eigenfunctions. JPEG image compression uses the discrete cosine transform, another operator, to separate an image into frequency components and discard the ones the eye can't see. Every digital filter in your phone's audio processing is an operator. The mathematics of operators is the mathematics of any system that transforms signals.

Conclusion

An operator is a transformation that takes a function and produces another function. Linear operators — like the derivative, the integral, and convolution — respect addition and scaling, making them mathematically tractable. Their eigenfunctions are the natural modes: the inputs that pass through unchanged except for scaling. The spectrum of possible eigenvalues determines the system's behavior — discrete for atoms, continuous for free particles. Operator theory is linear algebra promoted to the world of continuous signals, and it underpins every technology that processes audio, images, or physical measurements.
