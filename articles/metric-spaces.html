Metric Spaces: Generalizing Distance and Convergence

#151 of 156 November 21, 2026 Group 6 · Jul 2026–Jan 2027

Doctors comparing two patients' genomes want to know how "different" the two DNA sequences are. But DNA sequences aren't numbers — you can't subtract one from another.

What Makes Something a Distance?

You've used distance since childhood. The distance between two numbers on the number line, the distance between two points in a room, the distance between two cities on a map. What do all of these have in common? Three properties: distance is zero only between identical points, distance is symmetric (A to B equals B to A), and the triangle inequality holds (going directly is never longer than going via a detour).

A metric space is any set of objects — numbers, functions, DNA sequences, images, probability distributions — equipped with a distance function satisfying those three properties. The "space" can be anything. The "metric" is whatever sensible notion of distance applies.

Metric space: a set X with distance function d(x,y) satisfying: 1. d(x,y) ≥ 0, and d(x,y) = 0 iff x = y (identity of indiscernibles) 2. d(x,y) = d(y,x) (symmetry) 3. d(x,z) ≤ d(x,y) + d(y,z) (triangle inequality) Examples: - Real line: d(x,y) = |x - y| - Edit distance on strings: minimum insertions/deletions to transform one to another - Hamming distance on DNA: number of positions where two sequences differ - Earth mover's distance on probability distributions All satisfy the three axioms. All are metric spaces.

Convergence in Metric Spaces

Once you have a distance, you can define convergence: a sequence x₁, x₂, x₃, ... converges to a limit L if the distances d(xₙ, L) approach zero. This works in any metric space. A sequence of DNA sequences "converges" if they get closer and closer (by edit distance) to some target sequence. A sequence of probability distributions converges if they get closer and closer (by some distribution distance) to a limit distribution.

Most of the theorems about convergence on the real line — the ones you learn in calculus — hold in any metric space. Continuous functions (those that map nearby points to nearby points) behave similarly. Cauchy sequences (where the terms get closer and closer to each other) can be studied in any metric space. The entire infrastructure of analysis generalizes.

Completeness: No Missing Limits

A metric space is complete if every Cauchy sequence converges to a limit within the space. The real numbers are complete: any sequence of real numbers where the terms cluster together must converge to a real number. The rational numbers are not complete: the sequence 3, 3.1, 3.14, 3.141, 3.1415, ... gets closer and closer together, but its limit (π) is irrational — not a rational number at all. The sequence is Cauchy but the limit falls outside the space.

Completeness is what allows infinite processes to have definite answers. In a complete metric space, you can define limits of sequences, integrals as limits of sums, and solutions of differential equations as limits of approximations — confident that the limits actually exist within the space you're working in.

Back to DNA

The edit distance between two DNA sequences is a genuine metric. Biologists use it to build phylogenetic trees — family trees of species — by finding the tree structure that minimizes total evolutionary distance. Convergence in edit distance describes what it means for a population's genetic makeup to evolve toward a stable equilibrium. The triangle inequality says that the evolutionary distance from humans to chimps plus chimps to gorillas is at least the direct human-to-gorilla distance — a biological truth encoded in the metric axioms.

Conclusion

A metric space is any set with a well-defined notion of distance — satisfying three natural axioms. The same mathematical theorems that describe convergence on the real number line work in any metric space, whether the space is DNA sequences, images, probability distributions, or functions. This generality is the power of abstract mathematics: prove a theorem in the right level of generality, and it applies to every specific case automatically.
