PAC Learning: The Theory of What Machines Can Learn

#141 of 156 September 12, 2026 Group 6 · Jul 2026–Jan 2027

A child sees a hundred dogs and learns to recognize dogs she's never seen before. A spam filter sees a million emails and learns to catch spam it's never encountered.

The Learning Problem

The setup is simple. There's a concept you want to learn — say, "this email is spam." Examples are drawn randomly from some unknown distribution (the kinds of emails people actually send). A learning algorithm sees labeled examples (spam/not spam) and produces a hypothesis — a rule for classifying new examples. The question: how many examples do you need, and what makes a hypothesis "good enough"?

Valiant's insight was to make both "good enough" and "probably" precise. Perfect accuracy is impossible — you'll always face edge cases. But you can ask for high probability of high accuracy. That's the PAC criterion: Probably Approximately Correct.

PAC Learning Definition: A concept class C is PAC learnable if there exists an algorithm such that: for any ε > 0 (accuracy parameter, e.g., ε = 0.05 means 95% accurate) for any δ > 0 (confidence parameter, e.g., δ = 0.01 means 99% confident) for any distribution over examples the algorithm, given at least m examples where: m ≥ (1/ε) × (log(|C|/δ)) outputs a hypothesis h with error ≤ ε with probability ≥ 1-δ. In plain English: with enough examples, you get a guarantee — with high probability, your hypothesis will be nearly correct.

The Sample Complexity Bound

The most important result from PAC theory is the sample complexity bound: how many examples m you need to guarantee learning. The bound depends on two things — the complexity of the concept class (how many different concepts you might need to distinguish) and your accuracy/confidence requirements. More complex concept classes need more examples. Higher required accuracy needs more examples. Higher required confidence needs more examples.

For the spam filter: the concept class is "all possible spam-classifying rules." If the rule space is huge — millions of possible rules — you need more examples to guarantee finding a good one. If the rule space is constrained — say, only rules based on five specific words — you need far fewer examples. PAC theory gives you the exact tradeoff.

VC Dimension: Measuring Complexity

The key measure of a concept class's complexity is its VC dimension — named after Vladimir Vapnik and Alexey Chervonenkis. The VC dimension measures how complex a set of classification rules is by asking: what's the largest set of points that the rules can classify in every possible way?

For linear classifiers in 2D (drawing a line and saying "left is class A, right is class B"): any 3 points can be separated in all 8 possible ways, but no set of 4 points can always be separated. So the VC dimension is 3. Higher VC dimension means more expressive rules — but also means you need more examples to learn reliably. The fundamental theorem of PAC learning says a concept class is learnable if and only if its VC dimension is finite.

What PAC Theory Tells Us About Deep Learning

Modern neural networks have astronomically high VC dimensions — they can memorize any labeling of any training set if they're large enough. PAC theory's classical bounds would predict they need billions of examples to generalize. Yet in practice, a neural network trained on thousands of images generalizes to millions of new ones. This gap between theory and practice is one of the deepest open problems in machine learning — classical PAC bounds are too pessimistic, and researchers are still developing better theories for why deep networks generalize as well as they do.

Conclusion

PAC learning gave machine learning its theoretical foundation: a precise, mathematical answer to when learning from examples is guaranteed to work. The framework says you can achieve high accuracy with high probability — provided you have enough examples, where "enough" depends on the complexity of the concept class. For simple concept classes (low VC dimension), a few dozen examples suffice.
